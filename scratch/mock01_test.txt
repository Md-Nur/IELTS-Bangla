The Bicycle: From Wooden Hobby-Horse to Everyday Machine
Few inventions are as simple, or as influential, as the bicycle. More than a billion of them are thought to be in use
around the world today, carrying commuters, couriers and children to school. Its appeal lies largely in efficiency,
since a person on a bicycle uses less energy to cover a given distance than with almost any other means of travel. Yet
the machine took almost a century to reach its familiar form, and its early history is a story of false starts, eccentric
designs and a surprising amount of social change.
The ancestor of the bicycle appeared in 1817, when a German forester named Karl von Drais built a wooden frame
with two wheels set in line and a steerable front wheel. The rider sat astride the frame and pushed himself along with
his feet. Drais had been searching for a quicker way to travel around the estates he managed, at a time when bad
harvests had left oats scarce and horses expensive to feed. His ‘running machine’ drew crowds in Mannheim and
Paris, and fashionable young men in London nicknamed it the hobby-horse. However, it was awkward to use on the
rutted roads of the period, and pedestrians complained so often about riders on pavements that several cities banned
the machine from them. Public interest faded within a few years.
The next important step came in the 1860s in France, where pedals were fixed directly to the front wheel of a similar
frame. These machines, called velocipedes, were heavy and uncomfortable, and their iron-rimmed wheels earned
them the nickname ‘boneshakers’. Nevertheless, they proved that a rider could stay balanced while using his own
legs to supply power, and workshops in Paris began to produce them in large numbers. Visitors to the city carried the
idea home, and by the end of the decade riding schools, where beginners could learn to stay upright indoors, had
opened in several European and American cities. The craze did not last, however: the outbreak of war between
France and Prussia in 1870 disrupted production in Paris, and by the early 1870s enthusiasm for the velocipede had
cooled.
In the 1870s designers tried to make bicycles faster in the only way they could: by enlarging the front wheel. Because
one turn of the pedals produced one turn of the wheel, a bigger wheel covered more ground with each push. The
result was the ‘penny-farthing’, named after two British coins of very different sizes, whose front wheel could be
more than a metre and a half across. Riders sat high above the ground, and a sudden stop could throw them over the
handlebars. The machine was therefore popular mainly with young, athletic and wealthy men, and it was regarded as
a sport rather than a means of transport.
The breakthrough came in the 1880s with the ‘safety bicycle’. In 1885 John Kemp Starley, working in Coventry,
produced the Rover, which had two wheels of roughly equal size and a chain that drove the rear wheel. The rider sat
low, between the wheels, and could put both feet on the ground. Three years later a Scottish veterinary surgeon
named John Boyd Dunlop developed an air-filled rubber tyre for his young son's tricycle. It absorbed the bumps of
the road and made cycling far more comfortable, and within a few years the penny-farthing had almost vanished.
Starley's design was soon copied by rival firms, and Coventry became the centre of the British cycle industry.
The 1890s saw a ‘bicycle boom’ in Europe and North America. Factories adopted mass production, prices fell, and
for the first time ordinary workers could afford a machine. Manufacturers competed fiercely, and features that are
still found on bicycles today, such as ball bearings, brakes and gears, were improved during this period. Cycling clubs
flourished, and the new freedom of movement had unexpected effects. Women in particular found that the bicycle
gave them independence, and it encouraged them to wear more practical clothing, such as divided skirts. The
American campaigner Susan B. Anthony once remarked that the bicycle had done more to free women than anything
else in the world.
The car eventually reduced the bicycle's importance in wealthy countries, but it never disappeared. In the 1970s,
rising fuel prices and growing concern about pollution renewed interest in cycling, and some cities, notably in the
Netherlands and Denmark, began to build networks of separated cycle lanes. Today the bicycle is again promoted as
a clean and healthy form of urban transport, and electric models are extending its appeal to older riders and to people
who face long journeys over hilly ground.

Green Roofs: Gardens in the Sky
A In the centre of many cities the only unused land is overhead. Buildings in dense urban areas are crowned with
acres of flat roofing that is hot in summer, wasted in winter and invisible to everyone except the pigeons.
Increasingly, architects and city planners are asking why these surfaces should not be covered with plants. The idea is
not new: turf roofs have kept Scandinavian houses warm for centuries, and the Hanging Gardens of Babylon are said
to have been planted on terraces. What is new is the modern green roof, a layered system of waterproofing, drainage
and growing material designed to support vegetation on top of an ordinary building.
B The first benefit that developers notice is thermal. Dark roofing absorbs sunlight and radiates it back as heat, so
that city centres can be several degrees warmer than the surrounding countryside, a phenomenon known as the urban
heat island effect. A roof covered in plants reflects part of that light and uses much of the rest to evaporate water,
which cools the air. Measurements on buildings in several countries suggest that the surface of a green roof can be
much cooler than that of a conventional roof on a hot afternoon, sometimes by more than twenty degrees Celsius.
Cooler roofs in turn mean that the rooms below need less air conditioning, which saves electricity at the very time
when demand for it is highest.
C Water is the second advantage. In a heavy storm, rain falling on roads and rooftops rushes into drains all at once,
and in older cities with combined sewage and rainwater systems this can cause overflows of untreated waste into
rivers. Green roofs act like sponges. The growing material soaks up much of the rainfall, the plants take up some of it,
and what remains is released slowly over several hours. Engineers in Germany, where green roofs have been
encouraged by regulation and subsidies since the 1980s, estimate that such roofs can hold back between half and
three quarters of the water from an average shower.
D Wildlife also benefits. Even a thin layer of soil planted with sedum, a hardy succulent, will attract bees, butterflies
and beetles, and larger roofs with a deeper layer of soil have been colonised by ground-nesting birds. In some cities,
rare species that have lost their natural habitats now breed on rooftops. Ecologists point out, however, that roof
habitats are isolated islands, and that animals must be able to reach them from parks or gardens nearby if the roofs are
to make a lasting difference. Once insects have arrived, larger animals tend to follow, so that a single roof can come
to support a small food chain.
E None of this comes without cost. A green roof is heavier than a gravel one, particularly when the soil is saturated
with water, so older buildings may need expensive structural reinforcement before one can be installed. The initial
price, which includes waterproofing, drainage and plants, is typically higher than for a traditional roof, and the roof
must be inspected and weeded regularly. Critics argue that these expenses discourage private owners, who rarely see
the financial reward of cooler air or reduced flooding because such gains are shared by the whole city. A leak under a
layer of soil can also be difficult to trace, which makes some owners cautious.
F Supporters reply that the extra outlay is partly recovered over time. A roof protected from sunlight and frost
suffers less wear, and some studies suggest that its waterproof membrane may last up to twice as long as an exposed
one. Buildings with green roofs also need less energy for air conditioning in summer and less heating in winter, and
in some commercial districts they command higher rents. Cities have therefore begun to share the burden: Toronto,
for instance, now requires green roofs on large new buildings, while others offer grants to owners who add them to
existing ones. The calculation is not simple, since the benefits vary with climate, roof type and the price of energy.
G The future of the green roof may depend on what it is used for. Some of the most ambitious projects now grow
food: rooftop farms in New York, Singapore and Paris supply restaurants and local markets with herbs, salads and
honey. Others are open to the public as parks, which is valuable in districts where land for open space is almost
impossible to find. Building managers add that office workers will often eat lunch in a rooftop garden rather than at
their desks. Whatever their purpose, such roofs share one lesson, which is that cities need not choose between
building and nature if they are prepared to build upwards.

The Myth of Multitasking
Walk into any modern office and you will see people doing several things at once: answering emails while listening
to a conference call, glancing at a phone in the middle of a conversation, switching between a dozen open windows
on a screen. Multitasking has become a badge of honour, and job advertisements routinely demand it. The
assumption behind this enthusiasm is that the brain, like a computer with several processors, can run more than one
task at the same moment. Research in psychology and neuroscience suggests that this assumption is largely mistaken.
What we call multitasking is, in most cases, rapid task-switching. When two activities both demand conscious
attention, such as writing a report and reading a message, the brain cannot give full attention to both. It must shift its
focus from one to the other, and every shift carries a cost. In laboratory experiments, volunteers who alternate
between simple tasks, such as sorting shapes and then sorting colours, become slower and make more errors
immediately after each switch. Moving between tasks of quite different kinds seems to cost more than moving
between similar ones. The individual delays are tiny, often a fraction of a second, but over a working day they
accumulate. Some researchers have estimated that heavy switching can consume a considerable share of a person's
productive time, although such figures are rough and vary from one study to another.
The costs are not only measured in seconds. Studies of office workers who are interrupted by incoming email have
found that it can take many minutes to return to the original task with the same level of concentration, because part of
the mind stays occupied with the interruption. Psychologists describe this lingering effect as ‘attention residue’. In
addition, people who switch frequently tend to report higher levels of stress, perhaps because they sense that they are
falling behind with everything at once. Anyone who has returned to a half-written document after answering a
message knows the feeling of having lost the thread.
There are exceptions, and they are instructive. Some combinations of tasks do work, but usually because one of them
is so well practised that it requires almost no conscious thought. An experienced driver can hold a conversation, and
many people can knit while watching television. The difficulty arises when both activities are demanding or
unfamiliar. This explains why even hands-free phone calls can impair driving: it is the conversation, not the handset,
that draws attention away from the road.
A more surprising finding concerns who believes they are good at it. In one widely discussed study, participants who
said they often used several kinds of media at once performed worse, not better, on tests of task-switching and of
ignoring distractions than those who rarely did so. This does not prove that multitasking damages the brain; it may be
that people who are easily distracted are drawn to multitasking in the first place. Even so, the result challenges the
comforting idea that practice makes perfect, and it should make us wary of anyone who claims to thrive on constant
interruption. The researchers themselves were careful to add that the evidence so far is incomplete.
Employers who encourage multitasking should therefore think carefully. Many organisations have tried to reduce its
costs with simple measures: meeting-free blocks of the day, ‘quiet hours’ for email, and software that gathers
notifications and delivers them at set times. Some companies have gone further and discourage staff from sending
messages in the evening. Such policies are not a cure, since some jobs genuinely involve unpredictable demands, as
in emergency dispatch or customer service. Even there, however, researchers suggest that training staff to prioritise,
and to finish one task before starting another wherever possible, produces better results than praising those who
juggle.
None of this means that the human brain is poorly designed. Our ability to shift attention quickly was valuable to our
ancestors, who needed to notice a predator while they were gathering food. The problem is that modern technology
offers endless triggers for that instinct, and we have mistaken the reflex for a skill. Learning to work on one thing at a
time may feel unproductive at first, but it is likely to be more effective, and less tiring, than the constant switching
that so many of us have come to regard as normal. Anyone who doubts it could try switching off all notifications for a
single afternoon and seeing what happens to the quality of the work.
